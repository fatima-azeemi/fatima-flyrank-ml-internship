The Decay Cliff:
What Actually Predicts Content Decline

01 · Abstract

Abstract

Content teams need to know which pages are about to lose search traffic, before the traffic is gone. This paper asks which safe, publicly-observable signals — word count, content age, impressions, average position, click-through rate, and search demand — are associated with a page's trend_direction going into decline, across an anonymized 30,000-page snapshot. We frame it as binary risk scoring, build a rule-based baseline (STALE_HIGH_IMPRESSIONS), and compare it against a 6-feature Random Forest under an honest, leakage-checked, stratified 80/20 split. The rule baseline barely beats guessing (48.2% accuracy, F1 0.41); the Random Forest reaches 67.7% accuracy and a test ROC-AUC of 0.737, with train/test metrics close enough to rule out serious overfitting. The headline finding runs against editorial intuition: in this snapshot, younger and lower-volume pages decline more often than older, high-traffic ones — age and impressions are better read as an impact signal for prioritization than as a risk signal for prediction. The output is a ranked, reason-coded action queue for human review, with explicit no-go boundaries around what should never be automated.

02 · Introduction

The decision this supports

Every content team eventually faces the same triage problem: thousands of live pages, a handful of editors, and no reliable way to know which pages are quietly losing search visibility before the monthly report shows it. The default heuristic is simple — flag anything old and high-traffic for a refresh. It's intuitive, it's cheap to compute, and as this paper shows, it's a weak predictor of who's actually declining.

The unit of analysis is a single content page (content_id), one row per page, drawn from an anonymized snapshot with an observation window centered on March 2026. The decision this work supports is editorial prioritization: which pages should a human open first this week. The cost of getting it wrong runs in both directions — flag the wrong signal and editors burn hours rewriting pages that were never at risk, while pages genuinely losing ground go untouched. Human intuition doesn't scale to tracking dozens of interacting metrics across tens of thousands of pages; that's the gap this model is built to fill, as a decision-support layer, not a replacement for editorial judgment.

Fields used

Features (6)

word_count, content_age_days, impressions_90d, avg_position, ctr, search_volume — all confirmed available before the prediction point.

Label / Proxy

is_declining, derived from trend_direction == 'down'. Context-only fields (content_id, client_id) are kept for joins, never fed to the model.

Excluded, and why: trend_pct is a direct percentage-change measure of the outcome itself — feeding it in would be near-total label leakage. trend_direction is the label source, not a feature. Short-window fields that overlap the 90-day aggregates were dropped for the same reason.

Missingness & limits

word_count is missing on roughly 25.7% of rows; search_volume on roughly 8.2%. Both are imputed with the training-set median only (see Methodology). Two structural limits carry through every result below: pages younger than 90 days lack a full historical window, so their metrics are noisier by construction; and the 30-day and 90-day windows in the source data overlap in time, so treat same-page metrics as correlated moving averages, not independent draws.

04 · Methodology

Methodology

Framed as binary risk scoring: predict the probability that trend_direction == 'down', using only pre-outcome metadata and search-console-style signals.

Baseline

The Week-4 rule baseline flags a page STALE_HIGH_IMPRESSIONS when content_age_days ≥ 90 and impressions_90d ≥ 500, on the theory that aging, high-visibility pages are the ones losing rank. This rule is the bar the model has to clear.

Split & leakage discipline

  • Split first: an 80/20 stratified train/test split (random_state=42) is taken before any imputation touches the data, preserving the ~54.2% decline rate in both folds.
  • Train-only imputation: the median imputer is fit on the training fold only, then applied to the held-out test fold — no test-set statistic ever informs training.
  • Leakage hunt: Pearson correlation between every candidate feature and the target was checked against a 0.85 threshold; nothing approached it. Label-derived fields (trend_direction, trend_pct) and pure identifiers (content_id, client_id) are excluded from the feature matrix by construction, and that exclusion is verified programmatically, not assumed.

Model

A Random Forest Classifier (n_estimators=150, max_depth=8, min_samples_split=10, random_state=42) was selected over a single decision tree or linear model because the underlying metrics are heavily right-skewed and interact non-linearly — a forest handles both without hand-built transforms, and its feature importances stay interpretable for the report.

What the model leans on

The headline reversal

The rule baseline's whole premise is that older + higher-traffic = more decline. The data says close to the opposite:

Read this carefully: age and traffic volume aren't proof of safety for old pages, and they aren't proof of risk for new ones. Young, low-volume pages are naturally more volatile while Google is still testing where to rank them. Old, high-impression pages are still the highest-stakes pages to get wrong — they carry the largest absolute traffic to protect — even though they aren't the most likely to decline. Impact and probability are two different axes; the rule baseline conflated them.

Where the model is wrong

False positives cluster on high-traffic or older pages going through a temporary dip that later recovers — the model reads short-term noise as risk. False negatives cluster on quiet, low-impression pages with subtle drift, where the statistical signal is genuinely weak. Neither failure mode is safe to automate around; both are exactly what the human-review layer below exists to catch.

06 · Limitations

Limitations & honest framing

Rewritten in safe language: using an honest, leakage-checked, train/test-isolated split, the Random Forest observed a test ROC-AUC of 0.737. Traffic and age signals show a directional association with the decline label, useful as decision-support for prioritizing editorial review — not as causal proof that any specific edit will recover traffic.

  • Not causal. Nothing here demonstrates that adding words, updating a date, or refreshing a page causes traffic to recover. The Freshness-Multiplier-style claims common in this space typically come from editor-selected refresh sets, which carry selection bias: pages editors chose to refresh were probably already promising. This paper's correlations are observational.
  • Not a live-algorithm model. This never predicts Google's ranking algorithm, a core update, or a manual action. It predicts an internal label built from historical trend direction in one snapshot.
  • Cohort effects are unresolved. Older and newer pages may belong to different seasonal or topical cohorts. A true "decay cliff" claim needs the same URLs tracked longitudinally with a time-aware split; this snapshot is cross-sectional.
  • Ceiling on accuracy. ~67.7% accuracy and 0.737 ROC-AUC are real, useful signal above both baselines — and also proof that most of the variance in page-level decline is not explained by these six safe features. Treat every score as a prioritization aid, never a verdict.
  • Data-quality boundaries. Sub-90-day pages lack a full history window; overlapping 30/90-day metrics are correlated, not independent, observations.

01

Refresh content STALE_HIGH_IMPRESSIONS

Age ≥ 90 days, impressions ≥ 500, and elevated model risk probability. Not because these pages are statistically most likely to decline — they aren't — but because they carry the largest baseline traffic to protect. Confidence: high on impact, moderate on individual-page risk; verify manually before rewriting.

02

Review stale, low-traffic pages STALE_LOW_IMPRESSIONS

Age ≥ 180 days with low impressions. Audit for keyword misalignment or consolidation candidates rather than a straight content refresh — the traffic may never have been there to protect.

03

Monitor, don't touch LOW_PRIORITY

Newer or stable pages. This is the tier where the "young pages decline more" finding actually lives — resist the urge to rewrite pages under 90 days; that volatility is normal indexing noise, not a real signal to act on yet.

Before acting: required human check

An editor must confirm, per flagged page: (1) the page serves timeless/evergreen intent where "age" is meaningless, (2) any observed drop isn't a seasonal or campaign artifact, and (3) the page isn't already redirected or scheduled for deprecation.

The no-go list — never automate: mass AI rewriting or publishing without editorial sign-off; automatic canonical redirection, deprecation, or deletion driven solely by a risk score.

Monitoring & retrain triggers

Retrain on a rolling 90-day window, quarterly at minimum. Trigger an earlier retrain if the impression or content-age distribution shifts more than ~25% (platform migration, new vertical), or if weekly Precision@50 on newly-flagged pages drops meaningfully below this validation baseline — a sign the queue no longer tracks real decline.
